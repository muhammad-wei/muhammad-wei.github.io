Higher-Order Force

The Physics of Access — Rocket Equation, Δv & Cost to Orbit

Not a sector — the constraint every layer below pays for. Reaching low Earth orbit takes ~9.4 km/s of Δv, so the rocket equation makes most of a launcher's mass propellant; engine efficiency (Isp), staging and reuse set the price per kilogram — ~$65,400/kg on the Space Shuttle, ~$2,600/kg on Falcon 9 (CSIS, FY2021 dollars). Every drop in $/kg opens the layers above.

Foundation Pillars — Lower Layer
💰

Capital & Anchor Customers

Governments buy first and fund development: 53 government space budgets totaled ~$141B in 2025 (Space Foundation). Private capital follows — SpaceX's June 2026 IPO raised ~$75B.

⚖️

Policy, Spectrum & Law

The Outer Space Treaty (1967; 118 parties) bars national claims; the U.S.-led Artemis Accords (76 signatories) and the China–Russia ILRS (17 partner countries and organizations) anchor rival lunar coalitions. ITU rules make constellations deploy 10%, 50% and 100% within 2, 5 and 7 years.

🧑‍🔬

Talent & Supply Chain

Propulsion engineers, test stands, radiation-tolerant electronics, carbon composites and launch-site access. Export controls (ITAR, EAR) and sanctions decide who can buy from whom.

🌌

In-Space Economy Emerging

Making, fixing and computing in orbit: Varda's drug-crystal capsules, debris inspection (Astroscale), orbital refueling and data centers in space (Starcloud, China's Three-Body constellation). Lunar water ice could one day become propellant.

01 · The rule

The Rocket Equation

Δv = Isp · g₀ · ln(m₀/m_f): speed gained grows only with the log of the mass ratio. At an Isp of 300 s, reaching orbit's ~9.4 km/s in one stage would need a mass ratio of ~24 — about 96% propellant. That is why rockets stage.

Mass ratio ~24 → ~96% propellant (illustrative)
02 · Efficiency

Isp & Propellants

Specific impulse (Isp) measures how efficiently an engine turns propellant into thrust; higher Isp means more Δv per kilogram. In vacuum: kerosene/oxygen (F-1) 304 s · hydrogen/oxygen (RS-25) 452 s · Hall-effect electric (NASA AEPS) ~2,800 s, at tiny thrust.

Vacuum Isp: 304 s · 452 s · ~2,800 s
03 · Engines

Engine Cycles

Gas-generator engines (Merlin) are simple but dump the gas that drives their pumps; staged-combustion engines (BE-4, oxygen-rich) burn it in the main chamber; full-flow staged combustion (Raptor) pre-burns both propellants for higher chamber pressure.

Gas generator · Staged · Full-flow
04 · Cost

Staging & Reuse

Dropping empty stages mid-flight keeps each stage's mass ratio achievable; landing them cuts the cost. One Falcon booster (B1067) has flown 37 times; Super Heavy was first caught by its tower on Oct 13, 2024; New Glenn landed in Nov 2025, and Zhuque-3 landed China's first orbital booster on land in Aug 2026.

Most-flown booster: 37 flights
05 · Where

Orbits 101

Low Earth orbit hosts the ISS (~415 km up, one lap every ~90 minutes) and the broadband constellations; GPS flies in medium orbit at ~20,200 km; geostationary satellites at 35,786 km circle once per sidereal day (23 h 56 min), so they hover over one spot.

ISS ~415 km · GPS ~20,200 km · GEO 35,786 km
06 · Coming home

Re-entry & Landing

Coming home means shedding orbital speed as heat: ~7.7 km/s from LEO and ~11 km/s (25,000 mph) from the Moon, as Orion did on Artemis II. Ablative heat shields, parachutes and splashdowns — or engines, for propulsive landings and tower catches.

~7.7 km/s (LEO) · ~11 km/s (Moon)

Reusable Heavy Lift

Starship reached orbit for the first time on Sep 28, 2026, deploying 26 Starlink V3 satellites before cutting the flight short after an engine shut down. Rivals: New Glenn reflew a booster in April but exploded in a May pad test; China's Long March 10 flew a low-altitude test in February.

Megaconstellations & Direct-to-Cell

Starlink has 11,156 satellites in orbit and 12.0M subscribers (Jun 30); Amazon Leo (392 in orbit) targets limited service by end-2026; China's Qianfan and Guowang have 258 and 256. Orbital Refueling & Servicing

NASA's Starship lunar lander must be filled in orbit by 10+ tanker flights, and ship-to-ship transfer has not flown yet. Servicing is already real: Astroscale's ADRAS-J inspected a spent rocket stage up close (2024) before starting to de-orbit in 2026.

~$500M
space-sustainability revenue, 2025 (SIA)
🏭

In-Space Manufacturing

Varda's capsules make drug crystals in microgravity and bring them home through re-entry. W-5 landed in South Australia on Jan 29, 2026, and a sixth capsule launched on Mar 30.

6
Varda capsules launched, to Mar 2026
🧠

Orbital Compute — AI × Space × Energy

In the right orbit a solar panel yields up to 8× more and works nearly nonstop (Google), so firms are testing data centers in space: Starcloud-1 flew an NVIDIA H100 (Nov 2025), China's Three-Body constellation runs AI models on its first 12 satellites, and SpaceX merged with xAI (Feb 2026). Google projects launch could fall below ~$200/kg by ~2035.

<$200/kg
projected launch price, ~2035 — near parity with ground data-center energy cost (Google, projection)
☀️

Space Power — Solar & Nuclear

Solar is the main power source in orbit: over 90% of small satellites carry solar panels and batteries, and space-grade multi-junction cells convert 30–34% of sunlight (NASA). The ISS's eight new roll-out arrays — the last two going up on upcoming spacewalks — lift its capacity from 160 kW to ~215 kW. Farther out sunlight fades (Mars gets 43% of Earth's) and lunar nights last ~15 days, so NASA plans Lunar Reactor-1 (at least 100 kW) for 2030.

~215 kW
ISS power capacity with eight roll-out solar arrays (NASA)
Living in Space
🫁

Life Support

Machines make the air and water: life-support systems (ECLSS) generate oxygen, scrub out CO₂ and recycle water. With its brine processor, the ISS now recovers ~98% of the water crews bring — urine included; food is still shipped up.

98% water recovery (NASA, 2023)
🦴

Your Body in Microgravity

Without weight, bones and muscles waste: astronauts lose 1–1.5% of bone density per month at the hips, spine and legs, and body fluids shift toward the head. Crews exercise about two hours a day, which slows the loss but does not stop it.

−1–1.5% bone density a month
☢️

Radiation

Above the atmosphere, cosmic rays and solar storms raise cancer risk. NASA caps an astronaut's career dose at 600 mSv; Curiosity's measurements put a Mars mission — 180 days out, 500 on the surface, 180 back — at ~1 Sv.

Career limit 600 mSv · Mars mission ~1 Sv
🌅

A Day in Orbit

The ISS laps Earth every ~90 minutes — 16 sunrises and sunsets a day. Talking to home is near-instant from orbit, but a signal takes ~1.3 s each way from the Moon and ~3 to 22 minutes from Mars.

16 sunrises a day
🏠

Where People Live

The ISS has been crewed without a break since Nov 2, 2000 and retires in 2030. China's Tiangong, complete since 2022, received its first astronaut from Hong Kong with Shenzhou-23 (May 2026), which also began a planned year-long stay. Next come commercial stations: Vast's Haven-1 targets 2027, and NASA plans its station contract award for spring 2027.

ISS crewed since Nov 2, 2000
🌙

Living Off-World

The Moon has 1/6 of Earth's gravity and water ice in shadowed polar craters; Mars has 38% gravity and air at ~0.6% of Earth's pressure, yet MOXIE made 122 g of oxygen from it. Crewed landings are targeted for 2028 (NASA's Artemis) and before 2030 (China).

Crewed landings: 2028 (U.S.) · before 2030 (China)
Space Economy Snapshot — Oct 2026
01

A $686B Economy

The global space economy grew 12% to $686B in 2025; 79% of it ($544.3B) is commercial, and 53 government budgets add ~$141B (Space Foundation). By SIA's narrower count, satellites earned $303B — over half from ground equipment such as GNSS chips.

Commercial 79% · Governments ~$141B
02

Record Launch Cadence

Orbital launch attempts hit a record 329 in 2025, up 25% on 2024's 264; SpaceX flew 170 and China 92. 2026 is on a similar pace: 245 through Oct 14, against 240 at the same point of 2025.

2025: 329 · 2026 to Oct 14: 245 (McDowell)
03

Capital Goes Public

SpaceX merged with xAI in an all-stock deal (Feb 2026, ~$1.25T combined) and listed on Nasdaq on Jun 12, raising ~$75B in the largest IPO on record. Q2 revenue rose 92% to $7.8B; capex reached $18.37B, mostly for AI, and the net loss was $541M.

SPCX: ~$75B IPO, Jun 12, 2026

Milestones — Nov 2025 → Oct 2026
Oct 8–9

SpaceX buys 800 MHz spectrum; U.S. carrier stocks slide

SpaceX agreed to buy up to 14 MHz of paired 800 MHz spectrum from Grain Management, pending FCC approval. The next day Verizon fell 8.75%, AT&T 9.81% and T-Mobile 13.27%.

Why it matters: markets now treat direct-to-phone satellites as competition for mobile carriers, not just a rural add-on.

Sep 28

Starship reaches orbit for the first time

Flight 14 deployed 26 Starlink V3 satellites. After a vacuum Raptor shut down early on ascent, the ship came home after less than two orbits, about three hours after liftoff, instead of the planned ~10 hours.

Why it matters: orbit unlocks the next tests — ship-to-ship propellant transfer for NASA's lunar lander, and full Starlink V3 deployment.

Sep 5

Isar's Spectrum reaches orbit from Norway

On its second flight, the German startup's rocket reached orbit from Andøya and deployed its payloads — the first orbital launch from Western Europe.

Why it matters: Europe gains a commercial route to orbit beyond Kourou; it is the first milestone met in ESA's European Launcher Challenge.

Aug 23

Chang'e-7 misses its 2026 launch window

Four days after the probe and its Long March 5 were moved to the launch area, China's space officials said the mission does not meet launch conditions and cannot fly in this year's window. No reason or new date was given.

Why it matters: China's lunar south-pole survey — a step toward its crewed landing and the ILRS — slips by at least one window.

Aug 19

Zhuque-3 lands its booster on land — a first for China

On its second flight, LandSpace's rocket put a satellite in orbit and landed its first stage on legs in Gansu, about eight minutes after liftoff; a fire broke out after touchdown.

Why it matters: booster recovery is no longer a U.S.-only capability — a Chinese commercial company has landed an orbital-class booster; reflight is next.

Jun 12

SpaceX lists on Nasdaq in the largest IPO on record

Priced at $135, the shares opened at $150 and closed at $160.95, valuing the company at ~$2.1T; the offering raised ~$75B (~$85.7B with the over-allotment).

Why it matters: the space economy's largest company is now priced daily in public markets, a reference point for private space valuations.

May 28

New Glenn explodes during a pad test

Blue Origin's rocket was destroyed in a pre-launch static fire that also damaged Launch Complex 36, weeks after its first booster reflight (Apr 19) ended with an upper-stage failure. The company traced the blast to a main oxygen valve on a BE-4 engine.

Why it matters: New Glenn launches Blue Origin's Blue Moon landers, so the setback reaches NASA's lunar plans; the first Blue Moon cargo flight slipped to early 2027.

Apr 1–10

Artemis II flies four astronauts around the Moon Headline

Wiseman, Glover, Koch and Canada's Hansen traveled 252,756 miles (~407,000 km) from Earth — farther than any humans before — passed 4,067 miles above the lunar surface, and splashed down off San Diego on Apr 10 after 695,081 miles.

Why it matters: the first crewed lunar mission in 53 years cleared Orion for Artemis III (2027) and a south-pole landing scheduled for 2028.

Feb 2

SpaceX acquires xAI

The all-stock deal valued the combined company at ~$1.25T and set out plans for AI data centers in space.

Why it matters: launch, connectivity and AI compute now sit in one company.

Nov 5–25, 2025

Debris strike prompts China's first space emergency response

Shenzhou-20's return was halted after a suspected hit by tiny debris; its crew came home on Shenzhou-21, and Shenzhou-22 launched uncrewed on Nov 25 as the station's new lifeboat. Shenzhou-20 returned empty on Jan 19, 2026.

Why it matters: debris is now an operational risk to crews, not just satellites — and China showed it can launch a rescue ship within three weeks.
